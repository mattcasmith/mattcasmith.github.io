GTA VI's world is unpredictable and unprecedented

2026-11-22  Technology,   Design,   Off Topic

It was a surreal moment to hold the Grand Theft Auto VI box in my hands last week. As annoying as it was that there was nothing inside except a download code and a poster, the arrival of Rockstar’s sequel made me pause to reflect.

When GTA V came out in 2013, I had recently started at my first journalism job out of university, and played my pre-ordered Xbox 360 copy late into the nights until I finished it. Now here I was, 13 years and two console generations later – married, a homeowner, and in a completely different career.

That’s the reality of modern gaming. In an era of 4K graphics, 60-hour playtimes, and soaring expectations, complexity has pushed development cycles to a point where the biggest games can take more than a decade. It’s frustrating as a player, and a trend I think puts future console releases at risk.

A code in a box

I wouldn't be doing my job properly as a small-time blogger if I didn't also mention that there is no true physical release of GTA VI. The boxed edition contains only a download code – no disc. Rockstar will have its reasons for this – for example, leak prevention through greater control over pre-installs, and size (a PS5 disc holds 100GB of data and the download was approximately 210GB, meaning at least two discs would be required). But for now, this means that potentially the most culturally influential game in history will cease to exist the moment it is no longer downloadable from the Xbox and PlayStation servers at some point in the distant future.

But all that was forgotten for a moment when I held that box in my hand. It was so long since I’d played a new GTA game that it stripped back my adult cynicism and took me back to the excitement I felt as a teenager on release day. This game had to be different. Surely with all those years to work and a ten-figure budget, Rockstar must have produced something special?

Welcome to Vice City

While I always play through the story in GTA games, I’m more interested in the world and its rules. How well has Rockstar developed a sense of place, and how much fun is there to be had experimenting with the mechanics?

These are both reasons why 2008’s GTA IV remains my favourite in the series. Liberty City’s approximation of New York mimics its atmosphere so well that it felt uncanny to walk the streets when I visited the real city years later. That accuracy was the result of a painstaking development process that involved 250,000 photos and reviewing reports on NYC’s infrastructure. I wrote about that in more detail in my piece on the value of real-world experience.

I’ve never been to Miami, so I can’t make comparisons to the real locations or atmosphere, but what Rockstar has achieved with Leonida is impressive. This applies both visually – the game is sometimes hard to distinguish from real-life footage – and in terms of content. The map is bursting at the seams with points of interest, explorable interiors, and chance encounters.

In GTA games of old, NPC drivers would usually do one of three things when hassled: freeze, speed off, or try to fight you. In GTA VI, they’re far less predictable. Some even chase you and try to ram you off the road. Then there are more scripted events – I’ve been robbed for my bag, witnessed car accidents, found people messing with my parked car… There’s always something new.

It’s been said about games before, but GTA VI’s world is the first that truly feels alive. IV and V’s maps were detailed, but you could trust that NPCs would behave like NPCs. There were rules. In VI, you never know which pedestrian might have different ideas and do something unexpected.

Planning and execution

Another thing that GTA IV did better than its successor was providing the player with a consistent sandbox. The way fire spread, the way you could get the police to blame an NPC if you timed things just right, the way you could get away with crimes if there were no witnesses – it felt like a real world with rules, which would carry on existing with or without the player.

GTA V felt much more like a game – either as the result of design decisions or the limitations of the Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3 on its initial release. Shoot a gun in the desert? The police are suddenly on you. Stand next to an NPC for too long? For some reason they’re now aggressive. Everything felt geared towards giving the player action, even if it didn’t make sense in context.

Going in, I felt my long-term enjoyment of GTA VI depended on which of these philosophies Rockstar had chosen to continue with. The answer is… both. It feels as though VI’s mechanics sit somewhere between – far more grounded than V, but with more concessions in the name of fun than IV.

I’ve yet to progress far enough to unlock the heavy weapons to test out some real carnage, but I would say the biggest upgrade over past iterations is the crime system. Police are far more intelligent than in V, taking note of your outfit and car and forcing a change unless you want to be recognised.

Your pursuers have also lost their telepathic abilities. Crimes are witnessed, then reported, and then the police arrive and start searching for the perps. This introduces a new element of planning – it’s super satisfying to hide your face, park your car around the corner, hold up a store, and make a getaway before the police arrive with nearly no details known about your identity.

Police are no longer telepathic. Crimes are witnessed, reported, and investigated in a far more intelligent way that requires planning.

For the first time outside of scripted missions, the police system also factors into your activities in the open world. This requires a delicate balancing act. When you hold up a shop or rob a criminal compound, make enough noise and the police will be called in. Then you’ll need to weigh up the risk of delaying your escape against the reward of any extra cash you’re able to swipe.

Beyond crime, Rockstar seem to have taken a page from GTA V roleplayers’ book. People react to guns carried openly, so they must be stored in your car boot. Cars must be refuelled at gas stations, and damage has much more of an impact on driving. GTA VI is peppered with small, realistic touches that help to balance out the limitless player power from past outings.

On frame rates

The chief complaint levelled at GTA VI is that it runs at 30 frames per second, and sometimes drops below that. Having spent around 15 hours with the game, I can confirm that this is both true and largely irrelevant. The frame rate is mostly stable, only noticeably dropping in certain action-heavy sequences or under challenging lighting conditions in dense areas. Engineering is all about trade-offs, and while I'm sure we'll get a smoother version on PC or a future console, for now this feels like a price worth paying for a game with a level of scope and detail we've never seen before.

My life has changed significantly in the last 13 years, and GTA VI’s rumoured 50-hour campaign will take longer to finish than GTA V’s did. I’ll share some thoughts on the story in my Field Notes newsletter when I reach the end.

The kinds of systems on display in GTA VI tend to reveal their bones and limitations in time. With enough hours, the player learns how they work and how to exploit their flaws. It’s still too early to know how long GTA VI’s illusion will last, but I trust Rockstar. In 2026, you can still launch GTA IV or V, feel a sense of place, and have fun. Their quality transcends their age.

For now, I’m happy to enjoy that illusion as much as possible while it does last. Because in this moment, GTA VI’s Leonida feels like the most expansive, detailed, and content-packed game world I’ve ever set virtual foot in.
